User guide: Canada Food System Stress Test

The Canada Food System Stress Test is a free, interactive web page that shows how a shock to Canada's food system could unfold, and how the timing of a response could change the outcome. It was built at the Arrell Food Institute (AFI), University of Guelph, as a prototype for a long-term research program on anticipatory decision-making.

Prototype v0.7, 13 November 2026. This is a demonstration. Every event and statistic is a fact linked to its source. The results of the tests are examples from simple models, not forecasts. Warning levels are example settings chosen by the authors. Proposed leads and funding are proposals that no agency has agreed to.

1. What the website is for

Shocks to Canada’s food system are inevitable; crises are not. Since 2021, drought, floods, avian influenza, a national rail shutdown, trade duties, a cyberattack and a fertilizer price spike have all hit the system. Whether a shock becomes a crisis depends on buffers: the spare capacity that absorbs it. Each department and company sees its own part, but no one tests how shocks spread, combine or could be stopped early. This website shows how a national stress test could. It helps answer three questions before a crisis, not after it:

  1. Where is Canada exposed? Which links in the food system depend on one route, one buyer, one supplier or a few plants, and how much spare capacity they have.
  2. What could go wrong, and when would we know? Eighteen threats from inside and outside Canada, each with the warning signs to watch for.
  3. Is acting early worth it? For seven threats, a “what if” test compares acting early, waiting to react, and doing nothing.

Who it is for: officials at Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada, the Canadian Food Inspection Agency, Public Safety Canada, Global Affairs Canada and the provinces; industry and farm groups; international partners; and researchers and students. No background in modelling is needed.

What it is not: a forecast. The tests use simple models with stated assumptions, and each one lists which inputs are sourced facts and which are examples. Use it to explore and discuss when acting early makes sense, not to predict a specific loss.

2. How the site is organized

The front page offers two connected parts. The risk dashboard shows the whole system and what acting early would be worth. The decision game takes one threat from the dashboard, a rail shutdown, and puts you in charge of it.

2. The decision game

The decision game is a three-minute exercise. A Prairie grain rail line may close in 72 hours. You choose a plan, receive news on Day 5 and may change course, then see what happened by Day 35 and what it cost, in Canadian dollars, compared with waiting to react. The lessons page explains what drove the result, the extra cost of waiting, what the plan would have cost if the warning had come to nothing, and the chance of closure above which the plan pays for itself. It ends with a link back to the rail shutdown test in the dashboard, and the rail test links to the game.

A panel at the bottom of every page links to the rest of the site:

  • The research plan: how the prototype would become tested science: the question, how it fits the National Food Security Strategy and emergency management in agriculture, three parts of the work, the first pilot on Prairie grain transport, the stages of testing, and what we ask of partners.
  • How the tests work: how seriousness is judged, what warning levels mean, how the “what if” tests work and what they cannot do yet, the full list of threats with filters, and a tracer that follows any threat to the part of the system it hits.
  • Recent events: dated, sourced news and government decisions behind the example warning levels.
  • Granary Risk Assessment Dashboard: a companion Arrell Food Institute dashboard that rates weak points in Canada’s agri-food system.
  • Potential partners: the organizations we propose to work with. The list is not finalized; being listed does not mean an organization has agreed or endorses the prototype.
  • Food system narrative: Feeding a Fragile World, Evan Fraser’s essays on buffers, food and crisis.

5. Using the site to prepare a decision

  1. Start with the risk dashboard. In The system tab, find the link you are responsible for, and choose “See what affects it”.
  2. Read the indicators. How exposed is that link, how serious is it, and can it be fixed?
  3. Check the threats. Which threats hit that link, and what are their warning levels?
  4. Open a “what if” test. Set how bad the threat is, read the warning signs, and compare acting early with waiting to react.
  5. Check the timing. When is the best day to start, when does acting stop paying, and what would a false alarm cost?
  6. Look at the timelines. What happens if this threat arrives alongside others?
  7. Agree a plan. Take the printed summary to partners, and use the plan card as a starting point: when to act, who leads, what money is set aside, and how results will be reviewed.

Worked example: phosphate before seeding

  1. In the system, choose farm inputs. Among the indicators, Canada makes no phosphate fertilizer of its own, and urea prices jumped about 40% before planting in 2026.
  2. Among the threats, “Fertilizer supply cut” is at Watch. Open the test: What if phosphate fertilizer imports are cut before seeding?
  3. Set the import shortfall to 50%, sourcing to start on 21 January, and replacement to 70%.
  4. Read the warning signs: Watch on 11 January (disruption reported), Get ready on 16 January (a gap at seeding is projected), Act now by 7 March (the last day for imports to arrive before seeding).
  5. Compare the tiles: waiting to react on 7 March costs about $284M; acting from 21 January costs about $162M, including $55M of response costs, so about $122M less. Doing nothing would cost about $805M.
  6. Check timing and risk: half the possible benefit is kept until about 11 April, and a false alarm would have cost about $55M. Choose “What if sourcing starts in March?” to see how much of the saving disappears.
  7. In the Possible timelines tab, choose “Test this timeline” under “Conflict abroad, pressure at home” to see the fertilizer shortfall alongside a lost export market, a crowded rail system and a cyberattack, and what a shared plan is worth.

All figures are example results from the prototype with its default settings, not estimates.

6. Behind the scenes

How the prototype works today

The prototype is a single web page with no server or database. When you move a slider, small models written in the page itself recalculate everything in your browser. Each model follows the same logic: a threat passes through a weak point, draws down buffers, and causes losses once the buffers run out. Acting earlier slows the shock, refills a buffer or reallocates it, at a cost that is counted.

  • Rail or port shutdown: freight capacity drops to zero for the shutdown period, then recovers over two weeks. Losses grow once elevator storage and feed stocks run out.
  • Phosphate before seeding: tracks phosphate in Canada against the season's need. Replacement shipments take about 55 days and are worth less if they land after seeding. Fields with high soil phosphorus lose less yield.
  • Cyberattack on distribution: tracks store stock of perishable and shelf-stable food day by day as deliveries stop, including panic buying. Public messaging that dampens panic buying takes effect only once the response starts.
  • Avian influenza: a simple spread model counts infected farms; a coordinated response slows farm-to-farm spread.
  • Prairie drought: tracks feed for livestock against winter need as a dry season unfolds. Feed from other regions arrives about 30 days after sourcing starts. The model counts losses from feed shortages, such as forced herd sales; crop losses from the drought itself are not counted, because early action cannot prevent them.
  • Combined tests: add the selected threats together, then add extra loss for shared buffers and extra delay when agencies are stretched. They compare acting early (each threat acted on at its “get ready” sign) with waiting to react (each acted on at its “act now” sign) and with doing nothing. The overlap cost is a simple multiplier, not a model of shared resources over time; that is part of the research programme.
  • The decision game: a daily stock-and-flow model of one grain corridor, separate from the rail model. Farms deliver 100 thousand tonnes a day to elevators that start with 10 days of storage room, matching how fast elevators filled in past stoppages. Rail, reserved or booked trucking, and temporary storage each have lead times and confirmation rates; feed and food deliveries (15 thousand tonnes a day) either share what moves or get first call. A Day 5 choice can change only what happens from Day 5 onward; higher fuel costs apply only to transport activated from Day 5. Costs are counted in Canadian dollars: response spending (fees, storage at $20 a tonne, and a premium of $50 a tonne for reserved and $70 for short-notice alternative transport) and losses (late exports at $4 a tonne per day of backlog, farm deliveries turned away at $40 a tonne, and feed and food shortfalls at $100 a tonne). These rates are illustrative, anchored where possible to published figures listed in the exercise. The wait-and-react plan monitors and books trucking only when elevators fill.
  • Loss of an export market: tracks the share of affected exports that still find a buyer after a trading partner imposes prohibitive duties. Planned diversification and storage support redirect sales sooner; redirected sales earn a little less, and unsold stock loses value.
  • Workforce outbreak at meat plants: tracks national beef processing capacity as an illness spreads through plant workforces. Plants need minimum crews, so capacity falls faster than attendance. Workplace protections reduce absence only after they start, building up over about ten days.
  • Indicators, threats and warning levels: the indicators, threats, warning levels and recent events are stored in the page as a dated snapshot chosen by the authors. Nothing is fetched live.

The page is hosted free on GitHub Pages. Each change is recorded in the repository, so any earlier version can be restored.

7. How the full system would work

The full system would keep Canada’s food system at the centre and watch threats at home and abroad. Data and news come in; analysts weigh the evidence; each threat and indicator is updated; combined tests show how threats interact; and warning signs and plans agreed in advance turn that into decisions. Lessons from each event feed back into the data and models.

How the platform stays credible

  • People before alerts. Like established crop-monitoring and famine early-warning systems, warning levels change only after analysts weigh satellite data, ground reports and expert judgement together.
  • A regular cycle. A monthly review updates the indicators, threats and front page, with an immediate review for fast threats such as a cyberattack or a strike notice.
  • No single score. Each type of threat shows its highest warning level and the threats behind it, not a single national score that would count shared causes twice.
  • Two tiers. A public tier like this site, and a restricted tier for sensitive scenarios.
  • Learning. Every event is reviewed: did the warnings come early enough, did the plan work, and what would we change?

8. How effective could it be?

The tool's value depends less on predicting shocks than on giving agencies a shared, agreed plan for when and how to act once warning signs appear.

What the evidence supports. In livestock disease, modelling of the 2001 UK foot-and-mouth epidemic showed that the speed of control measures strongly shaped how large the outbreak became (Keeling et al., 2001; Tildesley et al., 2006). In humanitarian work, linking forecasts to pre-agreed triggers and funding lets aid reach people before a disaster peaks (Coughlan de Perez et al., 2015). Households that received cash before the 2017 Bangladesh floods coped better than comparable households that did not (Gros et al., 2019). Research on food supply chains shows how shocks spread through trade and production links, and that diverse supply sources buffer cities against shocks (Laber et al., 2023; Gomez et al., 2021; Davis et al., 2021).

What is still uncertain. Most evidence on anticipatory action comes from humanitarian settings in lower-income countries, not from Canadian supply chains. How much loss early action avoids in Canada has not yet been measured. The program's back-tests on 2020–26 events are designed to answer that.

Our expectation (to be tested, not yet shown). Early action should pay most where the shock takes time to spread, such as a fertilizer shortfall months before seeding or a disease moving between farms. For fast shocks such as a cyberattack, warning time is short, so most of the value comes from plans prepared in advance rather than from prediction.

Limits to keep in mind:

  • The current numbers are examples and the models are simple.
  • Large shocks are rare, so models are hard to validate statistically.
  • False alarms carry costs and can erode trust.
  • Much supply-chain data is held privately.
  • A warning is only useful if an agency owns it and has agreed in advance to act on it.

How effectiveness will be measured in the full program:

  • Lead time: how much earlier the tool warns of trouble than current practice.
  • Hits and false alarms: how often warnings are followed by real disruption, and how often they are not.
  • Avoided loss in back-tests: estimated savings had agencies acted on the tool’s warnings in past events, reported as ranges.
  • Usefulness to decision-makers: whether users find the warnings credible, timely and actionable, measured through structured feedback.
  • Uptake: whether warning signs and plans are formally adopted by agencies.
