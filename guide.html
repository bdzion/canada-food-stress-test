User guide: Canada Food Shock Stress Test

The Canada Food Shock Stress Test is a free, interactive web page that shows how a shock to Canada's food system could unfold and how much money and disruption acting early could save. It was built at the Arrell Food Institute (AFI), University of Guelph, as a prototype for a long-term research program on anticipatory decision-making.

1. What the website is for

The site helps people answer three practical questions before a crisis, not after it:

  1. Which shocks matter most? A ranked list of 15 real and emerging threats to Canada's food system.
  2. When do we need to act? For each modelled shock, the site shows the early signs to watch for and the point after which acting early stops paying off.
  3. What is acting early worth? The site compares three choices side by side: act early, act late, or do nothing.

Who it is for: officials at Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada (AAFC), the Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA), Public Safety Canada, Global Affairs Canada and the provinces; industry and farm groups; international partners such as IICA; and researchers and students.

What it is not: a forecast. Every number comes from a simplified model with stated assumptions, and each scenario lists which inputs come from published sources and which are illustrative. Use it to explore and discuss the logic of early action, not to predict a specific loss.

2. How the site is organized

The row of buttons at the top switches between six views. The number before a shock's name (#1, #3 and so on) is its rank in the shock register.

Each shock view has the same layout: a pathway strip across the top (shock, chokepoint, buffers, impact), controls on the left, four result tiles, and two charts. Under the charts, "Assumptions and sources" lists every input, and "Data table" shows the numbers behind the charts.

3. Key terms in plain language

TermPlain meaningExample on the site
ShockA sudden event that disrupts how food is produced, moved or soldA ransomware attack shuts down a grocery distributor
ChokepointA single point that a lot of food or inputs must pass through, so a problem there spreads widelyTwo railways carry most grain and fertilizer; Canada makes no phosphate fertilizer of its own
BufferA reserve that absorbs a shock for a while: stocks, storage, spare capacity, alternative suppliersStore shelves hold about 3 days of perishables; elevators fill in about 10 days
CascadeA shock spreading from one part of the system to the nextRail stops, then elevators fill, then processing plants shut, then exports are lost
ImpactThe harm that results once buffers run outEmpty shelves, lost crops, culled birds, lost sales

Readiness signalEvidence the shock is real and will spread; time to get ready and, ideally, to start actingThe rail corridor actually closes; five farms are infected
Action signalThe point where buffers are nearly used up; acting now is urgentElevator storage is full; perishables are about to run out
Early actionResponding at or soon after the readiness signalOrdering phosphate from other suppliers in January
Late actionResponding weeks after the readiness signalThe same order placed in March
Do nothingNo planned response; the shock runs its courseThe baseline every result is compared against
Decision windowThe period in which acting still saves a lot. The site marks it as closed after the last start date that avoids at least half of the avoidable lossFor the phosphate shock, about 11 April with default settings

4. Reading the results

Every result updates the moment you move a slider. Hover over any chart to see exact values.

ElementWhat it tells you
Orange tile: projected loss, no early actionThe cost if nobody responds
Blue tile: with action from day XThe cost if the response starts on the day you chose, split into remaining loss and response cost
Green tile: net loss avoidedThe saving from acting, and its share of the no-action loss. If acting is too late, it says so
Amber tile: decision window closesThe latest start that still avoids at least half of what could be avoided
Chart 1: how the shock unfoldsThe dashed orange line is the no-action path; the blue line is the path with your response. The shaded gap is the harm prevented. Dashed vertical lines mark the watch, ready and act signals; the solid blue line marks when your response starts
Chart 2: the cost of waitingFor every possible start day, the total cost of responding then. Where the blue curve rises to meet the dashed line, acting no longer pays. "Your choice" marks your current setting

Integrated view: line chartThe cost of the whole compound year as every response waits longer, with and without a joint playbook

5. A decision workflow

Steps 1 to 7 take about 15 minutes on the website; step 8 is where the results become a commitment that agencies and industry sign up to.

Worked example: phosphate before seeding

  1. In the Shock register, fertilizer and input supply ranks third.
  2. Open Phosphate before seeding. The pathway strip shows the chokepoint: Canada makes no phosphate fertilizer and relies on one main supplier.
  3. Set the import shortfall to 50%, sourcing to start on 21 January, and replacement to 70%.
  4. Read the signals: watch on 11 January (disruption reported), ready on 16 January (seeding gap projected), act by 7 March (last day for imports to land before seeding).
  5. Compare the tiles: about $805M lost with no action versus about $162M with action from 21 January, including response costs.
  6. The cost-of-waiting chart shows the window closing in mid-April. Click "What if sourcing starts in March?" to see how much of the saving disappears.
  7. In the Integrated stress test, add a rail shutdown and see how the overlap raises the total.
  8. Take the result to partners: agree that a projected seeding gap triggers alternative sourcing by a named agency, and write that into a joint playbook.

All figures are illustrative outputs of the prototype with its default settings.

6. Behind the scenes

How the prototype works today

The prototype is a single web page with no server or database. When you move a slider, small models written in the page itself recalculate everything in your browser. Each model follows the same logic: a shock passes through a chokepoint, draws down buffers, and causes losses once the buffers run out. Acting earlier slows the shock, refills a buffer or reallocates it, at a cost that is counted.

  • Rail or port shutdown: freight capacity drops to zero for the shutdown period, then recovers over two weeks. Losses grow once elevator storage and feed stocks run out.
  • Phosphate before seeding: tracks phosphate in Canada against the season's need. Replacement shipments take about 55 days and are worth less if they land after seeding. Fields with high soil phosphorus lose less yield.
  • Cyberattack on distribution: tracks store stock of perishable and shelf-stable food day by day as deliveries stop, including panic buying.
  • Avian influenza: a simple spread model counts infected farms; a coordinated response slows farm-to-farm spread.
  • Integrated test: adds the four shocks together, then adds extra loss for shared buffers and a response delay when crises overlap.

The page is hosted free on GitHub Pages. Each change is recorded in the repository, so any earlier version can be restored.

7. How effective could it be?

The tool's value depends less on predicting shocks than on giving agencies a shared, agreed plan for when and how to act once warning signs appear.

What the evidence supports. In livestock disease, modelling of the 2001 UK foot-and-mouth epidemic showed that the speed of control measures strongly shaped how large the outbreak became (Keeling et al., 2001; Tildesley et al., 2006). In humanitarian work, linking forecasts to pre-agreed triggers and funding lets aid reach people before a disaster peaks (Coughlan de Perez et al., 2015). Households that received cash before the 2017 Bangladesh floods coped better than comparable households that did not (Gros et al., 2019). Research on food supply chains shows how shocks spread through trade and production links, and that diverse supply sources buffer cities against shocks (Laber et al., 2023; Gomez et al., 2021; Davis et al., 2021).

What is still uncertain. Most evidence on anticipatory action comes from humanitarian settings in lower-income countries, not from Canadian supply chains. How much loss early action avoids in Canada has not yet been measured. The program's back-tests on 2020–26 events are designed to answer that.

Our expectation (to be tested, not yet shown). Early action should pay most where the shock takes time to spread, such as a fertilizer shortfall months before seeding or a disease moving between farms. For fast shocks such as a cyberattack, warning time is short, so most of the value comes from playbooks prepared in advance rather than from prediction.

Limits to keep in mind:

  • The current numbers are illustrative and the models are simplified.
  • Large shocks are rare, so models are hard to validate statistically.
  • False alarms carry costs and can erode trust.
  • Much supply-chain data is held privately.
  • A warning is only useful if an agency owns it and has agreed in advance to act on it.

How effectiveness will be measured in the full program:

  • Lead time: how much earlier the tool signals trouble than current practice.
  • Hits and false alarms: how often signals are followed by real disruption, and how often they are not.
  • Avoided loss in back-tests: estimated savings had agencies acted on the tool's signals in past events, reported as ranges.
  • Usefulness to decision-makers: whether users find the signals credible, timely and actionable, measured through structured feedback.
  • Uptake: whether triggers and playbooks are formally adopted by agencies.
